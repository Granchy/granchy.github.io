### Hardware Accelerator Primer
With the rise of artificial intelligence (AI), came the rise of hardware accelerators (you may have also heard of GPUs, NPUs – Apple, or TPUs – Alphabet). These are specialized pieces of computer hardware which are used to accelerate computations for tasks which are highly parallelizable. Okay what does that mean? Well, your central processing unit, or CPU, is used for general-purpose computing as it is good at accomplishing at lot of different tasks (hence why it is general-purpose), however it can only execute one instruction at a time.

So say I had to compute an addition of two vectors, take **A** and **B**. If I wanted to accomplish this using a CPU, I would have to individually add each element in the array, sequentially. If it takes the CPU one millisecond (in reality this is much faster) to add each element, and there are 100 elements in the array, it would take 100 milliseconds to complete the computation of **A** + **B**. Notice how with larger and larger sizes for **A** and **B** this can become extremely slow for a CPU. Therefore, in a world that is processing more and more data each day, we need something that is capable of executing these repetitive, monotonous computations quickly.

That is where hardware accelerators come in. Graphics processing units (aka GPUs or graphics cards), neural processing units (NPUs), and tensor processing units (TPUs) are capable of executing the same instructions at the same time, operating on different data. This is known as parallel processing and it becomes extremely useful in AI model training because the basis of AI model training is matrix multiplication and addition (so something along the line of **Y = A * B + C**). NVDA, AMD, AAPL, GOOG, and many other companies have developed their own designs for these hardware accelerators, and with the boom in AI, this hardware has been in high-demand as companies race towards AGI.

### NVIDIA - Key Success Factors
Nvidia is currently the dominant force in the hardware accelerator market as of writing this blog. Analyst estimates place Nvidia’s market share between 70% and 95% with no signs of slowing as AI adoption continues.

In my opinion, Nvidia’s success is largely attributable to two factors:

**1. Their rapid transition from gaming to AI**

Nvidia was and still is a key player in gaming prior to the rise of AI. Their original claim to fame was producing GPUs to improve the gaming experience of gamers playing graphics-intensive games. I lived this first-hand when my dad placed a GTX 1070 in my gaming PC which jumped my Fortnite gameplay from 60 fps to 200 fps (rip old fortnite :/). The math involved in accelerating the computations required to render high-quality graphics in games are very similar to those involved in training AI models. In the early 2010s, Nvidia’s CEO, Jensen Huang, noticed this opportunity and started transitioning his company away from solely gaming devices to delivering next-generation AI hardware. This enabled Nvidia to be a first-mover within the AI hardware space, allowing it to gain early market share over its competitors and starting, what is known today as, its dominance over the space.


**2. CUDA's Developer Experience**

Gaining market share is one-thing, but holding on to it is a separate issue. Nvidia has held onto an overwhelming majority of the AI hardware market since the AI boom began in 2022 with OpenAI’s release of ChatGPT. Some would attribute this success solely to Nvidia’s hardware, arguing that none of its competitors have comparable offerings in terms of quality. A case can be made for this, however, I don’t believe it’s a strong one. In the world of AI, hardware is only part of the battle, the other part being software. In Nvidia’s case, that software is CUDA. CUDA is what developers use to extract maximum performance from their Nvidia chips and considering Nvidia’s dominance in market share, it is the de facto standard in the high-performance computing industry. One of the main advantages CUDA has over its main competitor’s, AMD’s, counterpart (known as ROCm) is the developer experience. ROCm has been known to be riddled with bugs, and until as of recent, has not responsively tended to them, therefore deterring users from the platform and consequently AMD’s hardware. In contrast, CUDA, which was released in 2007, has had a longer lead-up to the AI boom which has allowed Nvidia to improve and polish the platform while growing a large community around it. Additionally, CUDA has first-class support in industry standard tools such as PyTorch, TensorFlow, and JAX which are Python ML libraries, and is often first at adopting new machine learning features, while ROCm often lags. As of recently, AMD has received advisory from SemiAnalysis, a semiconductor research firm, to develop a strategy to improve ROCm and catch up to Nvidia. AMD’s hardware does not lag significantly behind that of Nvidia’s, however to bridge the chasm between the two firms, AMD needs to deliver a better experience to its developers.

### Industry Look-Ahead
As frontier AI labs and the Magnificient Seven continue to pour billions into AI research, hardware, and data center buildouts, it will be interesting to see how demand for chips from different suppliers shifts. With its current dominance over the market, Nvidia is in a position to be price-setter. Until now, Nvidia has enjoyed this powerful position as it sees its bottom-line grow year-to-year and from one quarter to the next; and with its recent deal with Groq, an inference hardware provider known for its “Language Process Unit” or LPU, will hope to continue to strengthen this performance. However, the emergence of Google’s TPU, AMD’s MI300 line of GPUs and improvements to ROCm, as well as smaller players such as Cerebras, the AI hardware industry is ripe for a shift away from Nvidia, as frontier labs look to reduce their dependence on the hardware giant.
